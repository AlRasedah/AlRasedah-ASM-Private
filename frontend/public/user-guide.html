1. What is Exteriq ASM#

Exteriq ASM is a continuous External Attack Surface Management platform. It discovers everything your organization exposes to the internet — domains, subdomains, IP addresses, open services, web applications, certificates and cloud resources — keeps that inventory current, and tells you what changed, what is exposed, and what matters most.

Unlike a one-off scan, Exteriq ASM builds a living picture of your attack surface. Each scan is compared with what was seen before, so you get a chronological stream of changes: a new subdomain, a newly opened port, an expiring certificate, a vulnerability that appeared — or one that was fixed.

What you can do with it
  • Discover your internet-facing assets automatically, including shadow IT you did not know about.
  • Assess exposures and vulnerabilities, each scored 0–100 by practical risk (not CVSS alone).
  • Crawl and actively test web applications for vulnerabilities such as injection and cross-site scripting (see Web application testing).
  • Monitor for changes continuously and get alerted through email, webhooks, Slack, Microsoft Teams or your SIEM.
  • Report to executives and engineers with ready-made reports in HTML, PDF and CSV.

Authorized by design. Exteriq ASM only ever actively contacts assets inside an authorized scope you define. Every target is checked against that scope before any traffic is sent, and every decision is logged.

3. Signing in & the interface#

Signing in

  1. Open the Exteriq ASM web address provided by your administrator and enter your email and password.
  2. If two-factor authentication is enabled on your account, enter the 6-digit code from your authenticator app.
  3. Forgot your password? Use Reset password on the sign-in page; you will receive an email with a reset link.
Tip: Turn on two-factor authentication for your own account under Your account. Administrators can require it and can reset it for other users.

You are signed out automatically after a period without activity — 30 minutes unless your administrator changed it. The sign-in page tells you when that is why you are back there. Leaving a screen open does not count as activity, so an unattended workstation does not stay signed in.

Finding your way around

The left sidebar is your main menu. The top bar has two important selectors:

  • Organization selector — filters the whole app to one organization, or choose All organizations for a combined view.
  • Tenant selector — appears only if your account belongs to more than one tenant (workspace); switches between them.
  • The bell / alert count shows unacknowledged medium-or-higher changes; the account chip opens your account; the Documentation link opens this guide.

The sidebar groups screens into daily use (Dashboard, Inventory, Shadow IT, Findings, Changes, Scans, Reports), Configuration (Organizations & scope, Scan profiles, Integrations, Users, Settings, Audit log) and, for platform administrators, Platform (Tenants). Menu items you do not have permission to use are hidden.

5. Dashboard#

The Attack surface overview is your starting point — “what is exposed, what changed, and what matters most.” Every card is clickable and takes you to the matching filtered view.

  • KPI tiles: overall risk score, total & active assets, new assets in the last 7 days, unknown / shadow IT count, critical and high findings, known-exploited (KEV) findings, and changes in the last 24 hours.
  • Risk trend and attack-surface growth charts over the last 30 days.

If you have not created an organization yet, the dashboard guides you to do that first.

6. Organizations & authorized scope#

Before anything can be scanned you create an organization and define its authorized scope. This is the single most important safety control in the product: Exteriq ASM never actively scans anything outside the scope you authorize.

Adding scope

  1. Go to Organizations & scope and open (or create) an organization.
  2. Under Authorized scope, choose Add and enter your root domains, IP addresses or CIDR ranges — one per line.
  3. Decide whether to permit active scanning for these entries (port discovery, web fingerprinting, crawling and vulnerability testing). Leave it off for passive-only monitoring.

Wildcards. Authorizations are usually written as *.example.com, so that form is accepted: it is stored as example.com with subdomains included, covering the domain and everything under it. *.dev.example.com works the same way for a branch of the tree, and wildcards can be used for exclusions too. Pasting both example.com and *.example.com is fine — the second line widens the first entry instead of being rejected as a duplicate. The wildcard can only replace the first label, so a.*.example.com and *example.com are refused rather than guessed at.

Only add assets your organization owns or is explicitly authorized to test. Every addition is recorded in the audit log.

Per-entry options

  • Subdomains — for a domain entry, choose whether subdomains are included (a *. entry sets this for you).
  • Active scanning — toggle whether that entry may be contacted actively.
  • Ownership — verify you control a domain by publishing a DNS TXT record (below).

Verifying domain ownership

Click the ownership badge next to a domain to open the verification dialog. Publish the shown DNS TXT record at your DNS provider, then choose Check now. DNS changes can take time to propagate. Your administrator can make verification mandatory before any domain is actively scanned (see Settings).

IP addresses and ranges have no DNS record to publish, so when verification is mandatory a platform administrator approves them instead, with the Approve button next to the entry. Entries that were added while verification was switched off are not treated as proven: they show as unverified until verified or approved.

Non-public addresses — loopback, private networks, link-local (including cloud metadata addresses) and carrier-grade NAT — are never actively scanned, even when a name in your scope resolves to one. They still appear in the inventory, which is useful: an internal address in public DNS is worth knowing about.

Exclusions

Add exclusions for anything that must never be actively scanned (e.g. a sensitive host or a range you do not own). Exclusions always win over inclusions.

Scanning policy

  • Actively scan resolved IPs — also scan the IP addresses your in-scope hostnames resolve to (these may be shared hosting; turn off to restrict scanning to explicitly listed ranges).
  • Skip shared CDN/WAF edges — avoid port-scanning shared content-delivery / WAF addresses.

Scope checker

Use the Scope checker to test whether a specific host, IP, CIDR or URL would be authorized for active scanning, and why. Great for confirming coverage before a scan.

Deleting an organization removes all of its assets, findings and history and cannot be undone. Removing a scope entry keeps existing assets but stops scanning them actively.

7. Scans & profiles#

A scan runs a profile against an organization. A profile is an ordered pipeline whose stages always run in the same order: discovery → open-source intelligence → DNS → network ownership → ports → web fingerprinting → web crawling → vulnerability detection. A profile can include any subset of these.

Built-in profiles

ProfileWhat it doesContacts your hosts?
Passive DiscoveryFinds subdomains from passive sources and certificate transparency, resolves DNS and maps network ownership.No traffic beyond DNS resolution.
Standard ASMPassive discovery plus web-service fingerprinting, discovery of commonly exposed services, and safe exposure/vulnerability detection. Recommended for daily monitoring.Yes (active).
Deep AssessmentExtended discovery and port coverage plus the broader safe detection set, including informational exposures.Yes (active).
Web Application Scan (DAST)Crawls each web application and actively probes it for web vulnerabilities such as injection and cross-site scripting. See §12.Yes (active, intrusive).
Exposure MonitoringA fast re-check of already-known assets (DNS, web services, common ports). Suited to frequent schedules.Yes (active).
Administrators can also create custom profiles under Scan profiles, choosing which stages to include and their options. Options are validated strictly.

Running a scan

  1. Open Scans and choose New scan (or Run a scan from the dashboard).
  2. Pick the organization and a profile. The dialog lists the stages and warns if the profile sends active traffic.
  3. Optionally limit to specific targets instead of the whole scope: hostnames, IP addresses, or host:port for an application on a non-standard port (app.example.com:8580). Naming a port scans exactly that port — useful when the application does not sit on 80 or 443 — and leaves the host's other ports alone. Targets must still be inside your authorized scope.
  4. Choose Start scan. You are taken to the scan’s detail page, which refreshes live.
First scan: an organization’s first successful scan is its baseline. It builds the initial inventory and its changes are recorded but not alerted.

Scan detail

  • Pipeline tab — each stage with its status, authorized target count, targets rejected by scope, observations and duration. A long-running stage shows how long it has run and its time limit.
  • Changes tab — every change this scan recorded.
  • Authorization log tab — every target and whether it was allowed or rejected by scope, with the reason and whether it was active or passive. This is your proof that scanning stayed in bounds.
  • KPI tiles show new assets, changes, new/resolved findings and assets that went gone/closed.
  • You can cancel a running scan.
If a stage finds no authorized targets it is skipped. If a required tool is unavailable the stage fails; optional stages are skipped instead. A scan is partial if some stages failed, completed if all succeeded. A partial stage still records everything it found, but it never closes ports, retires assets or resolves findings — it cannot prove something is gone when it did not finish looking.
If a tenant is suspended, its queued and running scans are cancelled; nothing further is scanned until it is active again.

Scheduling

On the Scan profiles screen, Schedule on any profile sets it to run by itself. Choose how often (every day, every week, every month), the day, and the time in your timezone — for example every Sunday at 09:00. The schedules table shows each one in the same words, so you can check at a glance when a scan will next run.

Days 29–31 are not offered for monthly schedules: a scan set for the 31st would skip every short month. If you need a cadence the chooser cannot express (say, every six hours), the API accepts a cron expression directly.

8. Asset inventory#

Inventory lists every internet-facing asset discovered for your organizations, with ownership and risk.

  • Filter by search text, asset type, active/inactive status, approval status, findings severity, technology, network (ASN), tag, owner, business unit, minimum risk, and first-seen date. A Third-party toggle includes related third-party infrastructure.
  • Sort by clicking sortable column headers (Asset, Status, Owner, First/Last seen, Risk, Findings).
  • Columns: asset value & title, type, IP(s), activity status & approval status, owner, first/last seen, risk score, open-finding count and tags.
  • Bulk update — select rows to set approval status, owner, business unit, criticality, or add tags to many assets at once.
  • Export CSV of the current filtered view.

Asset detail

Click any asset to open its detail page, with tabs for Ownership & classification (owner, business unit, criticality, approval, tags, notes), Relationships (what it resolves to, hosts, serves, belongs to), DNS, Web endpoints, Findings, Timeline of changes, and Raw observations from each scan. Risk is explained factor by factor.

9. Shadow IT review#

Shadow IT lists active assets discovered outside your known inventory — their ownership is unknown and their approval unverified until you review them. This is where you triage “what is this, and should it exist?”

  1. Filter by asset type if you like; the count shows how many assets await classification.
  2. For each asset, choose a classification: Approve, Expected, Third party, Unauthorized or Decommissioned.
  3. Open the asset first if you need to inspect it before deciding.

Classifying assets keeps your dashboard’s “unknown / shadow IT” count meaningful and feeds the risk score (unknown and unauthorized assets carry extra risk).

10. Findings#

Findings are the vulnerabilities and exposures on your assets, prioritized by practical risk — not CVSS alone.

  • Filter by status (open by default), severity, category, CVE, Known exploited only (CISA KEV) and Unassigned, plus free-text search.
  • Columns: risk score, severity, finding title (with badges), affected asset, status, first/last seen and how it was detected.
  • Export CSV of the current view.

Badges you will see

critical high medium low info — severity;  KEV — listed in CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog;  DAST — dynamically confirmed by active web-application scanning (see §12), as opposed to passively observed;  unverified — reported by an external database, not tested (see below).

Confirmed findings vs. reported, unverified

The two buttons above the list separate what was tested from what was only reported:

  • Confirmed findings (the default) — issues a scanner actually observed on your asset.
  • Reported, unverified — issues an external database such as Shodan associates with the service version it saw. Nobody tested them, so they may be wrong (a backported patch, for example). They do not affect risk scores, reports or alerts. When a scan confirms one, it appears under Confirmed findings.

Finding detail & workflow

Open a finding to see its description, remediation guidance, evidence, references, CVE/CWE, CVSS and EPSS scores, and a full explanation of its risk score. Analysts can:

  • Change status along an allowed path — New → Investigating → Remediated / False positive / Accepted risk, and Reopen.
  • Assign the finding to a user and add comments; all activity is timestamped.
  • Accept the risk (with justification and an optional expiry date) — a separate permission.
Findings resolve automatically. When a later scan that fully re-tested an asset no longer sees the issue, the finding is marked remediated and a “vulnerability resolved” change is recorded. If it comes back, it reopens. A scan that did not finish cleanly (errors, a timeout, output cut short) never resolves anything — it is marked partial and cannot vouch for what it did not see.

11. Changes & alerts#

Changes is a chronological, security-relevant feed built by comparing each scan with previous observations — new assets and subdomains, DNS/IP changes, ports opening or closing, services and technologies appearing or changing, certificate changes/expiry, vulnerabilities detected/resolved/reopened, risk increases, shadow IT discovered, and more.

  • Filter by change type, minimum severity, acknowledged state and date; optionally include baseline events.
  • Acknowledge changes you have reviewed (individually or a whole page) to clear them from the alert count.

To be notified of changes (rather than reviewing them here), configure alert policies and channels.

12. Web application testing (DAST)#

Exteriq ASM can perform Dynamic Application Security Testing — powered by OWASP ZAP — on the web applications it discovers. This adds two capabilities to the pipeline:

  • Web application crawling — spiders each web application to map its pages and endpoints, expanding the known surface, and passively inspects the responses for hygiene issues (missing security headers, insecure cookies, information disclosure).
  • Active vulnerability scanning — actively probes the discovered pages for web vulnerabilities such as SQL injection, cross-site scripting and path traversal.

Running it

  1. Make sure the target web applications are inside an authorized scope that permits active scanning. Active testing is intrusive.
  2. Start a scan with the built-in Web Application Scan (DAST) profile (or a custom profile that includes the crawling and active-scanning stages).
  3. If the application listens on a port other than 80 or 443, put host:port in Limit to specific targets — for example app.example.com:8580. The profile's own port sweep only covers common web ports, so without this the application may never be found.
  4. Watch progress on the scan’s Pipeline tab; results appear as assets (crawled endpoints) and findings.

Testing behind a login

Most of an application is only reachable after signing in. In the Start a scan dialog, a profile with web application stages offers Sign in with a session cookie:

  1. Sign in to the application in your browser as the user you want the scan to act as (a test account with the rights you want exercised — not a personal administrator account).
  2. Copy the session cookie from your browser’s developer tools (for example PHPSESSID=abc123; security=low), or switch the selector to Authorization and paste a token such as Bearer eyJhbGci….
  3. Start the scan. The crawler and the active scanner send that header with every request to the authorized origin, so they see the application as that signed-in user.
The value is used for this scan only: it is stored encrypted, sent only to the authorized origin (never to any other host, even after a redirect), and erased when the scan finishes or is cancelled. It is never shown again in the interface or the API. The scan list marks such scans as authenticated.
Sessions expire, and scanning as a signed-in user is more intrusive: the scanner can reach forms and actions an anonymous visitor cannot. Start the scan soon after copying the cookie, use a test account, and prefer a staging environment for the first run. For recurring authenticated scans, store the credential once under Integrations → Data-source API keys (zap_auth) instead; a value given in the dialog overrides it for that scan.
The web application scanner works on one job at a time, so a DAST stage may wait briefly while another scan finishes with it. Each job gets a clean scanner session, and testing stays on exactly the authorized site — a redirect to another host is never followed.

The DAST badge

Findings confirmed by the active scanner are flagged with a DAST badge in the findings list and a “DAST · actively verified” badge on the finding detail. It tells you the issue was dynamically exercised against the running application, not merely inferred — a strong signal for prioritization.

Authorization & availability. Active web testing sends real attack-style requests. It only runs against scope that permits active scanning, and it stays strictly on the authorized host. The DAST engine is an optional component; if your deployment does not have it enabled, those stages are skipped. Ask your administrator if the Web Application Scan (DAST) profile is available in your environment.

12a. Threat Center#

When a serious vulnerability is announced, the Threat Center shows which of your assets may be affected, which have been checked, and what remains open. Advisories are written by your platform team and matched automatically against the inventory your scans have already recorded — publishing an advisory never starts a scan on its own.

  • May be affected counts every asset on the advisory's list. Confirmed means a verified finding exists. Checked — not detected means a completed check found nothing; it is not proof the asset is safe. Inconclusive means the check failed, was blocked, timed out or could not reach the asset.
  • Potentially affected and Version unknown come from product and version names only; banners can be wrong and fixes can be back-ported, so these are never shown as confirmed. Reported, unverified means only a third-party exposure database reported it.
  • Open an advisory to see each asset's evidence (what was seen and why it counts), linked findings, owner and remediation guidance. Export CSV downloads the list.
  • Analysts can tick assets and choose Check selected assets when an approved check exists. It runs as a normal scan: your authorized scope, active-scanning permission, quotas and scheduling all apply, and it appears under Scans.
  • Remediation (open, in progress, resolved, accepted risk, not applicable) is your team's own workflow and is kept separate from what scans report.

Platform administrators manage advisories and the list of approved checks under Threat Center → Manage advisories: drafts are invisible to tenants until published, and archiving keeps each tenant's history.

12b. Website screenshots#

A screenshot shows what a web endpoint looked like to an anonymous visitor, so you can recognise an application — a VPN portal, a login page, a default install — without opening it. It is a picture, not evidence of a vulnerability.

  • Open a web endpoint in the inventory and choose the Screenshots tab to see the latest image, when it was taken, the page title and the address it loaded, plus the last ten attempts.
  • Capture screenshot (analysts and administrators) visits the page once from your scanner. It needs the same active-scanning authorization in scope as web fingerprinting. States: waiting, capturing, succeeded, failed, blocked (not allowed by scope or policy) and cancelled. A failed attempt never removes the previous image.
  • Tenant administrators turn screenshots on under Settings → Website screenshots and choose on-request or weekly. If your platform has not enabled the capability yet, the page says so.
  • Only the latest captures per endpoint are kept (two by default), within your tenant's storage quota.

12c. Exposure map#

The Exposure map draws what your scans observed: domains, the addresses they resolve to, the ports and services on those addresses, the web applications they serve, and the findings recorded on them. It shows one organization at a time, starting from its domains or from any asset you pick (also available as a tab on each asset).

  • Click a line to see what it means, which capability observed it, when, and whether it is current, stale, no longer observed, or only reported by a third-party database.
  • Click a node for its details. A "+N" means more relationships exist; Expand loads them. Large maps are trimmed and say so.
  • The map is not an attack-path analysis: a line never means one asset can be used to reach another.

Why is Facebook in this list? It is not social media. Facebook runs a public certificate transparency search, and searching it for your domain finds subdomains that appear in issued certificates. It needs a free Facebook developer app (key format APP_ID:APP_SECRET), not a Facebook page or account.

Good keys to start with: SecurityTrails, Censys, Shodan, VirusTotal, GitHub, Chaos and a certificate source (Cert Spotter or Facebook). The regional engines only help if you have assets hosted in China.

Key formats matter. Some providers need two parts — for example APP_ID:APP_SECRET (Facebook), API_ID:SECRET (Censys) or HOST:KEY (Intelligence X, ZoomEye). The form shows the expected format for the source you pick; a key in the wrong format is ignored by the source.

Shodan: more than subdomains

With a Shodan key stored, scans also look up every authorized IP address in Shodan's database and record what it last saw there: open ports, service products and versions, TLS certificates, reverse DNS names, the hosting network — and the CVEs Shodan associates with those versions. This is passive: the platform queries Shodan, never your hosts, so it also covers scope where active scanning is not authorized.

Because that data is historical, it is used carefully: it adds what is new but never marks an asset as “seen now”, never revives an inactive asset and never closes anything. Shodan's CVEs appear under Findings → Reported, unverified and stay out of risk scores, reports and alerts until a scan confirms them.

15. Settings#

Tenant-wide policies (administrators). Changes apply to all organizations in the tenant.

  • Asset lifecycle — how many consecutive scans can miss an asset before it is considered gone, per asset type, and how long to keep assets that are never seen again.
  • Scanning governance — require proof of ownership before anything is actively scanned. Only real proof counts: a DNS TXT record for domains, or a platform administrator's Approve for IP ranges. Entries added while the setting was off are marked unverified when you switch it on. Your platform may enforce this for every tenant, in which case it cannot be switched off here.
  • Email delivery (platform administrators) — the mail server used for password resets, invitations and alert emails: host, port, username, password, sender address and TLS. Send test email checks it immediately. No server access or restart is needed, and this replaces the deployment's environment settings.
  • Risk scoring weights — tune the points added for each risk factor and the score thresholds for each risk level. Saving recomputes scores.
  • Built-in detection rules — enable/disable checks for risky ports, management interfaces, certificate problems, weak TLS and exposed API documentation, and set the certificate-expiry warning window.
  • Report branding — set the brand name and accent colour used on generated reports.

16. Users#

Administrators manage team members under Users: invite/add users, set their role, deactivate or remove them, and reset another user’s two-factor authentication when needed. You manage your own account from Your account rather than the Users list.

17. Your account#

  • Change password — changing it signs you out of all sessions.
  • Two-factor authentication — confirm your password, set up a TOTP authenticator app, then verify a code to enable it. Disabling requires your password and a current code.
  • Email alerts — have changes emailed to your own login address. This is on by default for high and critical changes; you can raise or lower the severity, include the first (baseline) scan, or switch it off. You never type an address: it is always the address you sign in with, and you only receive events from the tenant you belong to. If no mail server is configured yet, your choice is saved and used as soon as one is.
  • API tokens — create tokens for automation (SIEM, SOAR, CI). A token never exceeds your own role; copy it when created because it is shown only once; revoke it anytime. For safety, API tokens cannot change account security: password, two-factor and token management need a normal sign-in.

18. Audit log#

Administrators can review a tamper-evident audit log of every meaningful action: who did what, when, from where. Each entry can be opened to see the before/after detail. The log is chained so any gap or tampering is detectable, and the screen confirms whether the chain is intact.

19. How risk scores work#

Every finding and asset gets a 0–100 risk score (higher is worse), grouped into levels: critical high medium low info. The score is explained — open any finding or asset to see exactly which factors contributed.

Scores combine, among others:

  • Severity of the underlying issue.
  • Known exploitation — listed in CISA KEV, or a high EPSS probability of exploitation, or a public exploit available.
  • Exposure — reachable from the internet, a management interface exposed, authentication exposed, or a risky port open.
  • Context — unknown / shadow IT or unauthorized assets, and how new the asset is.
  • Age — long-standing unresolved issues.

Asset risk rolls up from its findings and from related assets, so a risky endpoint raises the score of the host and domain above it. Administrators can tune all weights and thresholds in Settings.

21. FAQ & troubleshooting#

A scan stage says “skipped — no authorized targets.”

Nothing in scope matched that stage. Add the relevant scope, permit active scanning, and (if required) verify domain ownership. Use the Scope checker and the scan’s Authorization log to see why targets were rejected.

I ran a scan but see no changes.

The first scan is a baseline: it builds inventory and its events are hidden by default. Enable Include baseline on the Changes screen to see them, or run a second scan to see real differences.

A finding disappeared.

It was likely resolved automatically — a later scan that fully re-tested the asset no longer saw it. Filter findings by All statuses to see remediated items; check the asset’s timeline for the “vulnerability resolved” change.

The Web Application Scan (DAST) profile is missing or its stages are skipped.

The optional DAST engine may not be enabled in your deployment, or the targets do not permit active scanning. Ask your administrator, and confirm the scope allows active testing.

Nobody was notified about an important change.

Notifications require an alert policy that matches the change’s severity/type and points at an enabled channel. Check the Delivery log for errors, and remember baseline events are excluded unless a policy opts in.

An asset shows as “unknown / unverified.”

It has not been classified yet. Review it under Shadow IT and set its approval status.
